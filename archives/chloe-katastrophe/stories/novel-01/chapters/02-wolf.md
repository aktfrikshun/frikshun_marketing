# Chapter Two — Wolf

**Narrator:** Human Chloe  
**Related artifact:** *Wolf*  
**Status:** Rough prose v0.1  
**Calendar position:** 2022, shortly after Isabella and Chloe leave Russia  
**Canon effect:** None; communications and scene details remain proposals

> I will not make your death behave.

## Draft

The first version of my father's death arrived at 4:17 in the morning.

The second arrived at 4:26 and contradicted it.

By breakfast, we had five.

This is how truth enters during a war: late, breathless, and wearing someone else's coat.

I was awake when the phone vibrated. Sleep had become one of those quaint habits practiced by people whose fathers were not maintaining civilian infrastructure inside a country invading its neighbor.

The room belonged to my mother's sister, technically, although the wallpaper belonged to 1993 and the mattress belonged to an international tribunal. We had been back on the South Jersey shore for less than a week. Two suitcases remained open on the floor because closing them would have meant admitting we had arrived.

Rain moved against the window in soft Atlantic bursts.

My phone lit the ceiling.

The message was in Russian.

There had been an incident involving a repair convoy. Gregor Volkov's team had failed to report. Communications were intermittent. Further information would follow.

No greeting. No signature I recognized. No useful definition of incident.

I read it twice.

Then a third time, because repetition is the prayer of people who do not believe in prayer.

My father's name remained the same.

Everything around it changed.

I called the number.

The call failed.

I copied it into three search fields, checked the country code, searched the message text, searched pieces of the message text, searched the name of the agency that had not signed it. Nothing.

At 4:21 I sent Papa a message.

Are you safe?

At 4:22 I deleted safe and wrote alive.

At 4:23 I deleted that too.

Where are you?

One check mark appeared beside the message.

Not delivered.

I sat on the edge of the bed and listened to the house.

My mother was awake.

I knew because she had stopped pretending not to be.

Across the hall, a floorboard gave one careful click. Then another. Isabella had spent years entering rooms as if doors had personally offended her. Silence required effort.

I opened the first message again.

Incident.

A beautiful word. Polished. Bloodless. You could place it in a report without getting any on your hands.

The phone vibrated.

The second version said the convoy had reached its destination but come under attack during an emergency communications repair. Several personnel were injured. Names unavailable.

Reached its destination.

Under attack.

Injured.

Three improvements, if one ignored that none belonged to the same story.

My mother opened the door.

She wore one of Papa's white shirts over black leggings. The sleeves were rolled twice, badly. Her dark hair was tied behind her head, except for the sections that had declared independence during the night.

"Who is it?"

I turned the phone facedown.

A ridiculous gesture. Isabella could detect concealed disaster through drywall.

"Work."

"Whose?"

I looked at her.

She crossed the room and held out her hand.

I gave her the phone.

Her eyes moved across the first message, then the second. She read both again. Her face did not collapse. That would have been kinder. It became still.

"Call him."

"I did."

"Again."

I called.

The connection produced half a ring, a scrape of static, and nothing.

She took her own phone from the pocket of the shirt and called. The same nothing answered her, but apparently it sounded more insulting in Italian because she swore at it accordingly.

"He should have come," she said.

It was not the first time.

It was not even the first time that morning, although the morning had only just begun.

I watched rain gather on the glass.

"He believed hospitals would lose power."

"He believed he was the only man in Russia who understood electricity."

"No. He believed he was one of the people responsible."

"And who was responsible for you?"

The question struck exactly where she intended. My mother did not weaponize love often. When frightened, she became an arms dealer.

"He helped us leave."

"He put us on an airplane."

"Yes."

"He stayed."

"Yes."

"Do not explain your father to me, Chloe."

"I wasn't."

"You use that voice when you explain."

"What voice?"

"His."

That silenced both of us.

Another message arrived.

The third version did not mention a convoy. It said an infrastructure team had been sent to restore power serving a hospital and nearby emergency shelters. Contact had been lost after an explosion.

My mother read it over my shoulder.

"Which hospital?"

"It doesn't say."

"Where?"

"It doesn't say."

"Who sent them?"

"It doesn't say."

"Then it says nothing."

"It says someone wants us to think hospital."

She turned toward me.

I heard Papa in the question before she asked it.

"Why?"

"Because hospital makes staying sound noble."

"It was noble."

"It was stupid."

"Those aren't opposites."

She slapped the phone onto the bed.

"Do not."

"Do not what?"

"Turn this into one of your arguments."

"I am trying to find out what happened."

"No. You are trying to make what happened obey rules."

The rain hardened against the window.

I wanted to tell her that facts were not rules. Facts were the only parts of the world that did not change because we were afraid of them.

It would have been a very Gregor thing to say.

So I did not say it.

My mother sat beside me. The mattress surrendered several inches.

For a while we listened to the storm and the small domestic noises below us: a refrigerator cycling, pipes knocking, my aunt moving through the kitchen without coming upstairs. The house knew before anyone told it. Houses are excellent gossips.

At 5:03, a man from Papa's office called.

He addressed my mother formally and asked whether she was somewhere private.

She laughed once.

It was the worst sound I had ever heard.

I stood and went to the window, giving her the illusion of privacy inside a room where I could hear every word.

There had been damage to a communications site.

Or an electrical substation.

The translation shifted depending on which technical noun the man chose and how badly the connection tore it apart.

Papa had joined an emergency team.

The route had become unsafe.

There had been shelling nearby.

No, the man could not confirm whose.

No, he did not know whether the team had been targeted.

No, he had not seen the site.

Yes, someone had seen the vehicle.

No, that person was not available.

There were no confirmed survivors.

There were no confirmed fatalities.

Gregor was missing.

Gregor was presumed—

The call dropped.

My mother held the phone away from her face.

"Presumed what?"

The blank screen declined to testify.

She called back. Nothing.

Again.

Nothing.

She threw the phone.

It struck the wall, fell onto the carpet, and remained offensively intact.

I picked it up.

"Do not tell me it isn't broken," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"It should be."

"Yes."

She stood and began dressing properly. Jeans. Sweater. Boots.

"What are you doing?"

"Going back."

"To Russia?"

"To him."

"The airports—"

"I know what airports are, Chloe."

"The routes are closing."

"Then I will find one that isn't."

"And if the message is wrong?"

"Then he will be angry that I came."

"And if it isn't?"

She stopped with one boot in her hand.

There are questions that seek answers. There are questions designed to hurt anyone foolish enough to provide one.

I looked away first.

At 5:41, the fourth version arrived through a family acquaintance in Omsk. He had heard Papa was alive and being transferred.

My mother seized it.

"Where?"

I asked the acquaintance.

He did not know.

Transferred from where?

He did not know.

Who told him?

A friend who knew someone attached to emergency operations.

A friend with a name?

The messages stopped.

Hope is not gentle. It entered the room with muddy boots and made demands.

My mother called airlines.

I began building a timeline.

At the top of a blank document I typed every fact we could support.

Papa had reported for work.

Papa had joined or been assigned to an emergency team.

A system had failed or been damaged.

Communications with the team had been lost.

Everything else went into a second column labeled CLAIMS.

Convoy attacked.

Hospital power.

Communications site.

Substation.

Explosion.

Shelling.

Injured.

Alive.

Transferred.

Missing.

Presumed.

I stared at the list.

The claims did not assemble into a story. They circled an absence.

At 6:12 my aunt came upstairs carrying coffee no one had requested. She set it on the dresser and wrapped both arms around my mother.

Isabella resisted for three seconds.

Then she folded.

I had seen my mother angry, laughing, drunk, terrified, sunburned, victorious, and once attempting to remove a bat from an apartment using a saucepan and religious profanity.

I had never seen her become small.

I left the room.

The hallway walls held family photographs from summers we had spent here. My American childhood, delivered in seasonal installments.

There I was at six, furious about sand in my shoes. At nine, holding a fish with the expression of someone betrayed by biology. At fourteen, taller than my grandmother and pretending not to be pleased.

Papa appeared in none of them.

He had always been working.

Next year, he said.

When the project ends.

When the application clears.

When the system is stable.

Systems, I was beginning to understand, are only promises with cables attached.

At 6:38 the fifth version arrived.

This one included a document.

It bore an official header, a registration number, and three stamps. Bureaucracy's ceremonial costume. The text expressed regret. It referred to loss of life during the performance of essential duties. It named Gregor Volkov.

It did not state where he died.

It did not state when.

It did not state how his identity had been confirmed.

It did not explain why he had been missing forty-five minutes earlier and dead now.

I checked the metadata.

The document had been created before the first message reached us.

I checked the registration number against two public records. The sequence was plausible.

Plausible is a vicious word. It does not mean true. It means the lie remembered to wear shoes.

My aunt found me sitting on the stairs.

"Chloe?"

I handed her the phone.

She covered her mouth.

Behind her, the kitchen television showed buildings burning beneath a red banner. The sound was muted. Men in suits moved their mouths around certainty.

My mother came into the hallway.

She saw my aunt's face.

"No."

No one had spoken.

"No."

I stood.

She backed away.

The document remained open between us, my father's name dark against the white screen.

"No. They do not get to tell me with a form."

"Mama."

"Do not call me that like I am already alone."

I crossed the space between us.

She struck my shoulder once with the flat of her hand. Not hard. An objection filed against reality.

Then she held me so tightly I could feel her heartbeat through Papa's shirt.

"He should have come," she said into my hair.

I closed my eyes.

I wanted to defend him.

I wanted to accuse him.

I wanted to ask whether duty was still noble when it left your daughter holding an official document with no body, no witness, no place, and no final word.

Instead I held my mother while the television burned silently behind her.

Papa had taught me that belief was not knowledge.

That morning, I hated him for it.

Knowledge would have been clean.

Knowledge would have had coordinates.

Knowledge would have told me what he saw in the last bright second, what he trusted, what he doubted, whether the human voice got out.

All we had were stories arriving faster than evidence.

Convoy.

Hospital.

Substation.

Shelling.

Transferred.

Presumed.

Dead.

By eight, relatives had begun calling. By nine, strangers were sending condolences. By ten, Papa had become brave, loyal, murdered, sacrificed, foolish, heroic, Russian, anti-Russian, a servant of the state, a victim of the state, proof of one argument, and proof of its opposite.

Death had not made him simpler.

People had.

I returned to the document on my computer.

Under FACTS, I added:

An official notice identifies Gregor Volkov among the dead.

Then:

The notice does not establish the circumstances.

Then:

We do not know.

It was the only honest sentence in the file.

I opened another document.

For the title, I typed CATASTROPHE.

The English spelling looked too neat. Too clinical. It described an event from a safe distance.

I replaced the C with a K.

KATASTROPHE.

Now it looked foreign in both languages.

Appropriate.

I typed my name beneath it.

CHLOE KATASTROPHE.

I did not know whether it was a title, an accusation, or the name of the person who would have to exist after Chloe Volkov stopped knowing how.

My cursor blinked at the end.

A small vertical pulse.

Still here.

Still here.

Still here.

I saved the file.

## Reconstruction notes

### Decisions made in this draft

- Human Chloe receives several contradictory accounts of Gregor's death while staying with Isabella's South Jersey family.
- Chloe responds by separating supported facts from claims.
- Isabella's grief is physical and confrontational; Chloe's becomes investigative.
- The official notice confirms death without resolving place, cause, witnesses, or recovery.
- "Katastrophe" first appears privately as the title of Chloe's evidence file and a possible identity, not yet a public modeling name.

### Canon proposals introduced

- The family receives Gregor's death notice shortly after reaching South Jersey.
- A sequence of conflicting reports includes the already documented possibilities: repair convoy, hospital power, communications, explosion, and shelling.
- Chloe discovers that the official notice predates the family's first informal message.
- The spelling "Katastrophe" begins as a private rejection of tidy official language.
- Isabella possesses and wears one of Gregor's shirts after leaving Russia.

### Protected uncertainty preserved

- No exact date, place, mechanism, attacker, body recovery, witness, or final communication is established.
- None of the contradictory versions is promoted as objectively true.
- Gregor remains a civilian protecting infrastructure, not a combatant.
- Gregor and Isabella's disagreement remains morally unresolved.
- The scene does not prove conspiracy; wartime chaos and bureaucracy remain sufficient explanations.

### Continuity review needed

- Confirm whether Isabella and Chloe should already be in South Jersey when notified.
- Confirm whether an official death notice exists in this form.
- Decide whether the metadata discrepancy is meaningful, routine bureaucracy, or later narrative evidence.
- Decide whether this is too early for Chloe to type "Katastrophe," even privately.
- Determine whether the wolf pendant should appear physically in this chapter or enter later.
